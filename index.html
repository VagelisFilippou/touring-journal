Vented front brakes for the Touring, Alpina style (part 1)

A used vented-brake package rebuilt on the kitchen table: ti struts, E21 323i hubs with oversize studs, restored ATE 2002 Turbo calipers, Neue Klasse shields cut to scoop air like Alpina did, and NOS BALO discs.

When27 Sep – 25 Oct 2026

The idea

The 02 came with solid front discs; only the 2002 Turbo had vented ones. My Touring has the stock ti/tii front end (solid discs) and 250 mm rear drums with 19 mm wheel cylinders, without a brake pressure regulator. My father once tried 22 mm wheel cylinders with a regulator and did not like the brake balance, so he went back to 19 mm without one.

I weighed three ways to get vented front discs: E21 323i hubs with vented discs; Alpina-style discs (reproductions exist, but the ti/tii calipers then need spacers); or Turbo hubs and discs. The E21 323i route made the most sense financially: new Turbo hubs alone are expensive, the Alpina parts are expensive because so few are made, while E21 hubs, discs (ATE, Zimmermann) and parts are still easy to find. I also decided against aluminium hubs: they are dimensioned like the steel ones, so I did not trust them to be engineered for aluminium. When I discussed it on the bmw2002faq forum in August 2025, several members said they run the same combination.

I could not find a pair of E21 hubs in my father's warehouse, and a more urgent brake problem came first (see Maintenance, September 2025), so the project waited. In September 2026 I bought a used package with most of what it needs, and in October I rebuilt it so it can go straight onto the Touring.

What came in the box

The package, bought in September 2026: two ti/tii struts, two E21 big-bearing hubs already drilled for the 02's four-stud pattern and fitted with 2002 rear-hub knurled studs, two used vented discs, two four-piston calipers with the right spacing, and a pair of handmade aluminium shields from the previous owner.

Struts

The struts had been powder-coated years ago by the previous owner and were still in good shape, so they only needed a very thorough clean. I measured the distance between the caliper mounting points to confirm that they really are ti struts: ti/tii/Turbo struts have the wider 88.9 mm caliper mounts, the "small" 02s 76.2 mm.

Hubs: new studs

The hubs had radial play at the studs: the knurled studs no longer sat tight in their holes. I cleaned the hubs ultrasonically and derusted them completely, then pressed in oversize knurled studs, Febi 33499 (M12×1.5, grade 10.9), which grip properly in the worn holes. No press needed: I pulled each stud in with a conical wheel nut. First with the conical side against the hub, so the stud centres itself in the hole, then turned round to the flat side with a couple of greased washers underneath. After that: 1K etch primer and 1K black.

Why the old studs came loose: they were 47 mm long with a short knurl (about 5.5–6 mm long, Ø14.0 mm), so they gripped only part of the 10.8 mm hub flange, and the holes had worn to 14.00–14.20 mm. The Febi studs have a slightly bigger (about 14.25 mm) and longer knurl. Every one needed real force to go in and seated flush and square, much tighter than the old ones. With an old disc fitted they stand about 26 mm proud of the disc face. On the car they still get torque-seal marks and a re-torque (about 100 Nm) after the first drives.

Checking run-out

With the hubs on the struts I checked the run-out of the hub face with a dial indicator: about 0.05 mm on one hub and around 0.04 mm on the other. That is the full swing of the needle (total indicated run-out), good for 50-year-old hubs. The check that really counts comes later: the run-out of the new disc face once it is fitted.

Calipers: new for the car, old ones restored for sale

At Veterama in October I found a restored pair of original ATE four-piston calipers of the wide, spaced type for vented discs (E3/E9/2002 Turbo) in excellent condition. The restorer judged the pistons and bolts good enough to keep. The finish is a metallic plating: it looks like nickel, but zinc is possible, and I have not confirmed it. These go on the car.

The old calipers from the package were stripped completely, derusted in citric acid in the ultrasonic cleaner, and wire-brushed. The pistons cleaned up well. They are original ATE castings with the spacers, structurally complete. The plan: rebuild them with ATE piston and seal kits and new ATE bleed screws (three per caliper), paint them silver-grey with Foliatec two-component caliper coating, pressure-test them, and sell them as a properly rebuilt pair together with the previous owner's aluminium shields, rather than handing them in as cores.

Shields: Neue Klasse, Alpina style

Alpina fitted Neue Klasse disc shields on the 02 because their shape scoops air onto the disc. I bought a new pair (BMW 34 11 2 760 114), trial-fitted them to the struts, marked them, and cut them with a Dremel 4250 and reinforced cutting discs at about 15,000–20,000 rpm (one disc per shield), then deburred and painted them black. They sit symmetrically behind the strut mounting points; at their outermost point they reach about 145 mm from the centre, only about 5 mm beyond the caliper (about 140 mm), so the vented disc stays largely open. The previous owner's handmade aluminium shields were stripped and painted black; they will go with the old calipers.

Alpina mounted the Neue Klasse shield from the inner side of the strut, so its lip sits right against the vented disc and feeds air into it. Gert Hack’s book How to make it faster shows the period installation.

Discs: NOS BALO

The used discs from the package were checked, but for the car my father found a pair of new-old-stock BALO discs for the E21 323i: BALO 02038 = BMW 34 11 1 119 111 (34 11 1 117 038), made in West Germany, unused and still in the box. Before fitting: remove the storage oil, check for storage corrosion, measure the thickness, check the run-out on the car and bed them in.

Test fit with the Borrani wheels

On 25 October I mocked up one corner with the Borrani CMR 6J×13 steel wheels I have, to check that everything fits before it goes on the car.

  • The Borrani 6J×13 clears both the ATE Turbo caliper and the modified NK shield.
  • The 26 mm stud projection is enough: the open wheel nuts show plenty of thread engagement, with the studs sticking out slightly beyond them.
  • Nothing appears to touch the wheel centre or the inner barrel.

Still to check: both sides, through several full turns with the nuts tightened evenly, aiming for at least 3 mm of clearance (5 mm or more preferred), and again with the BALO discs. The nuts used for the mock-up were fine for checking, but the final nuts must have the right seat for the Borrani wheels.

Where it stands

By 22 October both corners were mocked up: strut, hub, Turbo caliper and shield. The project is not finished yet. Still to do:

  • New FAG wheel bearings (713 6671 00) with their own races: the old bearings went through the ultrasonic and citric-acid cleaning with the hubs, so they will not be reused. The old races have to come out first (internal puller, a weld bead, or a workshop).
  • Fit the yellow-zinc hose clips and the M12 wave washers (Kapteijn). The zinc-plated brake-hose brackets are already on.
  • Make new CuNiFe brake lines.
  • Pads: Textar (already on the shelf), with a new Bosch pad fitting kit.
  • Rebuild, paint, pressure-test and sell the old calipers.
  • Fit everything to the Touring.

The ti struts currently on the car, with calipers restored in September 2025, then stay on the shelf as a plug-in alternative. Part two of this story follows when the brakes are on the car.

Two ideas for later: a removable wheel-speed sensor bracket made from a shield offcut (for data logging and wheel-slip measurements), and a Porsche split clamping spindle nut instead of the castellated nut and cotter pin, so the bearing play can be set exactly (seen on Uli Aichholz's car; Porsche 911 341 673 00, M18×1, still to check against the BMW spindle thread).

Twin Webers in two weeks, just in time for Voula

An untouched pair of Weber 40 DCOE 76/77 from my father's warehouse, rebuilt and fitted under an Alpina air box in two weeks, finished the day before the Voula hillclimb.

When8–24 Apr 2026
Photos25
Ledger SKUP-0216, P-0215, P-0217, P-0165, P-0096…P-0103, P-0106, P-0107

The deadline

The Voula hillclimb was on 25 April 2026, and I wanted the Touring there on twin side-draught Webers instead of the original single Solex carb of a 2002 2.0. That left about two weeks, and it turned out to be a hell of a job. I did it together with my father, in his yard in Athens.

The carbs

The carbs are an original, untouched pair of Weber 40 DCOE 76/77 that turned up in my father's warehouse. They cost nothing, but they needed a full rebuild before they could go anywhere near the engine.

Rebuilding them

I stripped both carbs completely and cleaned every part thoroughly. Every jet, emulsion tube and small part went into its own cup so nothing got mixed up between the two carbs. They went back together with new gasket sets. The spindle bearings were in excellent condition, so I left them in.

The jetting is the factory setting for the 40 DCOE 76/77 (from the Weber application table for the Alfa Romeo 2000, a 1962 cc four-cylinder like the M10): choke 32, auxiliary venturi 4.5, main jet 135, air corrector 210, emulsion tube F34, idle jet 55 F17, starter 85 F9, pump jet 35, needle valve 150.

Manifolds, gaskets and coolant

The intake manifolds were cleaned and polished; no port matching was needed. Between head and manifold went new paper gaskets, and between manifold and carb new phenolic spacers with two paper gaskets each (Walloth & Nesch). All washers and nuts are new. The manifold-to-carb nuts are 13 mm instead of the original 12 mm, because we could not find genuine 12 mm ones from BMW. That makes tightening the carbs onto the manifolds a bit trickier.

For the coolant I used a cleaned original 2002ti water divider. The metal bypass tube that a twin-carb engine needs came from an E30 318i M10 with L-Jetronic, with its one small outlet plugged. This way the heater hose on the firewall stays exactly as it was, so going back to a single carb would be easy.

Linkage and throttle

The original 2002ti throttle linkage was in excellent condition and went straight back on. The bridge-type bracket that bolts onto the manifold studs was raised with two thick washers, so the linkage clears the chokes. The DCOE-specific lever arms I did not have; I bought them separately from Athanasakos.

The firewall bracket that supports the linkage got a new bushing, softened in warm water to fit, which took the noise away. A throttle return spring runs from the left carb lever to the stud on the side of the manifold, as on an original 2002ti.

The rod from the pedal box to the carb linkage had to be straightened. The ti has its own firewall bracket, which pushes the rod to the side, and a bent rod then hits the gearbox and clutch. The ti originally has a straight rod, but we could not find one. The clamps at both ends of the rod are new.

The Alpina air box

The air filter housing came with the original Alpina A1 Solex DDH set I bought; we restored it in Delft in February, and it now carries Alpina lettering from Walloth & Nesch. The same set also gave the polished intake funnels and a second linkage. They are needed because the air filter tubes are inclined, and they make the connection between carbs and box. The box is held on one of the strut tower studs with a bracket that is also original to the A1 setup.

Into the car

On 20 April the old induction came off, the ports were covered, and the manifolds and carbs went on. The next days were for the linkage, fuel lines and the air box.

Finished, one day early

By 24 April the Alpina box sat on its bracket and the engine bay looked as if it had always been that way. The next morning we drove to Voula.

Later that summer: the right distributor

In the summer I fitted a good original Bosch 0 231 151 008 (JFU 4), the centrifugal-only distributor of the 2002 ti and tii. It was my father's old stock, kept aside for this car. I set it up following the factory manual and Wolfgang's guidance on the 02-club forum:

  • At idle the timing is at TDC.
  • 25° BTDC at 2,200 rpm, with the vacuum hose disconnected. (For the 0 231 151 008, -009, -003, 0 231 188 002, -001 and 0 231 129 037; only the 0 231 129 026 reaches 25° already at 1,400 rpm.) On the pulley that is about 25 mm from the TDC mark.
  • Full advance of 30–34° BTDC at 3,500 rpm, about 32 mm from TDC on the pulley.

The settings for a standard ti apply to engines up to a 304° camshaft.

How it runs

In general it behaves well and gives a good baseline. There is some spitting back through the carbs at low load around 2,200 rpm, so the first thing to try is a slightly richer idle jet: from 55 F17 to 60 F17.

Bringing a Ferrero wooden steering wheel back to life

Grey, brittle and coming apart at the old glue joint. Re-glued with epoxy and twine, sanded to 1000 grit, many coats of tung oil and a German wood wax.

When6 Feb – 15 Mar 2026
Photos21
Ledger SKUP-0210
Forumbmw2002faq thread

Where it started

I had this wooden Ferrero wheel for a while and always planned to restore it. It is a three-spoke wheel with polished slotted spokes and the engraved Ferrero signature. In August 2025 I asked on the bmw2002faq forum whether anyone knew more about it, but I could not find much information anywhere. The wood was not in good shape: grey, dry, and cracked along the joints.

It came apart, luckily

In January the hub and horn contact came off. On 7 February I started on the rim: the old finish came off with steel wool and sanding. While I was removing old epoxy from the wood near the spokes, the wheel split in two. Fortunately the only crack was at the joint between the two wooden halves, which was a big relief. The original glue had become very brittle and crusty over the years, and the halves had already separated. Underneath the wood there is a thin metal hoop on the spokes, and the wood is two grooved half-rims glued around it from either side.

Gluing it back together

I removed as much of the old glue as I could and sanded everything. Then the three parts, the two wooden halves and the metal rim, went back together with two-part epoxy, pressed together the old-fashioned way: dozens of turns of twine pulled tight all around the rim, and then waiting.

Filling and sanding

In a few places, especially where the wood meets the spokes, there were gaps. I filled them with wood dust mixed into epoxy. After curing, the excess came off with a razor blade, and then the sanding started again, working up gradually to 1000 grit.

Oil and wax

The wood then got many coats of tung oil, each one deepening the colour, with the wheel hanging up to dry between coats. The final finish is a German wood wax (it smells great), buffed with fine steel wool. The spokes were lightly polished up to 2000 grit and finished again with very fine steel wool.

The result

It was a lengthy process, but I am quite happy with the result. The horn button will need re-plating at some point; that will probably happen when I send out a larger batch of parts for plating.

I shared the finished wheel in the bmw2002faq steering wheel thread in March 2026 (link below).

Refreshing a 2002ti air filter housing for twin carbs

A solid but tired 2002ti air box: etch primer and satin black, Alpina-style open filters instead of the snorkels, and new mounting hardware made to carry them properly.

When28 Jan – 1 Mar 2026
Photos13
Ledger SKUP-0165

Why this part

For the twin-carb setup on the Touring I wanted the original 2002ti air filter housing. This one came with the original Alpina A1 Solex DDH set I bought, together with the intake funnels, a mounting bracket and a second linkage. It was solid but tired: chipped paint and surface rust.

Paint

I stripped it wherever needed, applied etch primer and refinished it in satin black. I did not want it to look too glossy.

Alpina-style filters

Instead of going back to the factory snorkels, I decided to try the "Alpina-style" open-ended filters. The snorkels just clip on, so it is fully reversible.

Hardware made to carry them

The larger filters need proper support, so I made a central threaded rod for them. Large penny washers and rubber isolators spread the load so the backing plates do not deform. The housing now sits on its bracket on the factory rubber mount (BMW 16114080148). The filters are held by knurled nuts with spring washers: secure, but removable without tools.

Weber or Solex?

When the housing was finished I was still deciding between Weber 40 DCOE and Solex 40 DDH carbs. In the end the Webers won: in April it went onto the Touring, with Alpina lettering, on top of a pair of Weber 40 DCOE 76/77 (see the twin-Weber story).

The rubber boots between the housing and the carbs are the original ones, reused.

Restoring a 2002 ti "silver dollar" cluster, 220 km/h

A complete, original but very tired early ti cluster brought back to the factory look: old black paint off the hubs, Alclad rings, satin housing, about 20 hours of work.

When5 Jan – 17 Mar 2026
Photos17
Ledger SKUP-0218, P-0171
Forumbmw2002faq thread

A spare from my father's ti conversion

This is a 2002 ti "silver dollar" cluster, the 220 km/h version that goes with the 3.64 differential. It is a 1971 unit and the spare my father kept from back in the day, when he upgraded the Touring to ti specification and fitted a 3.64 differential. It was complete and original, but cosmetically very tired: oxidised rings, hubs painted black, dusty inside and a generally flat look. Mechanically it was fine, so the goal was not to over-restore it but to bring it back to a factory-correct look.

Taking it apart

The cluster was completely disassembled. Rings, lenses, gauges and housing were separated and cleaned one by one.

The hubs: old black paint off

At some point the silver centres of the speedometer and tachometer had been painted black, unevenly. Painting the hubs of early clusters black was apparently common at the time, probably to make them look like the later clusters (or an Alpina). The fuel gauge and the rest were still stock, just dull with age.

Getting that old paint off was the hardest and slowest part, and honestly a real pain: with white spirit, without touching the needles and without damaging the rest of each instrument, because the chrome rings had been painted black too. Then I masked each instrument carefully and refinished the hubs with Tamiya XF-16 (Flat Aluminum). The aim was the slightly soft, frosted factory tone: not bright chrome, and not a glittery metallic.

The rings: gloss black and Alclad

The outer rings were the biggest visual change. After some tests with ordinary metallics I used a gloss black base (Tamiya X-1) and then Alclad Polished Aluminum. The gloss base is essential for depth and for the Alclad to behave. At first I was not happy with how the gloss black laid down (some orange peel), but I carried on carefully and the end result turned out very nice: bright but restrained, much closer to the original than typical hobby metallics. Alclad gives a beautiful finish, but it needs a very smooth surface and is fragile while it cures (don't ask me how I know).

The housing

The same technique went on the outer edge of the housing. The housing itself was cleaned and refinished in satin black (Humbrol 85). I deliberately avoided a glossy finish and kept the original texture.

Lenses, glass and needles

The lenses were cleaned and polished to remove the haze, not replaced, except in the tachometer, where the old plexiglass of the clock was replaced with real glass from a spare. The needles were cleaned and refreshed with AE-9 light grey, keeping their original tone. The retaining rings for the glass were refinished too: satin black on the front, light grey on the back.

Back together

Everything was then reassembled carefully, step by step. In total it took about 20 hours, most of it removing the old paint, masking, and cleaning the airbrush after every colour.

Lit up

Renewing the white paint inside the housing, which reflects the light behind the gauges, made a real difference. No LEDs: the original incandescent bulbs.

I shared the restoration on the bmw2002faq forum, where it got a very kind reception. I am now considering fitting it in the Touring: I think these early clusters are the best-looking ones.

A second cluster

In February I bought a second 220 km/h cluster on eBay and later restored it the same way. In March the two were lit up side by side. This second one is the cluster that went into the Touring on 2 August 2026, fitted with the speedometer from my father's cluster, which I had set to zero during its restoration. So the car's ti cluster counts from 0 km.

That also solved an old puzzle. The cluster my father had fitted was an original early 2002 tii cluster, made for the 3.45 differential of the early tii, which is why the speedometer read a little high with the 3.64. That tii cluster now has the speedometer from another tii cluster I bought, so its gearing is correct, but its kilometres no longer match the car. (A third early ti cluster came in a lot with other clusters and Touring C-pillar grilles; that one is still untouched.)

BrakesSKU P-0141, P-0150, P-0151, P-0153, P-0154
  • The problem: driving a bit spiritedly on mountain roads in Greece, the car suddenly pulled hard to the left.   • Cams (forum): a 285° cam gives little over stock; 300°/304° (e.g. Schrick) is a good street compromise with twin carbs.
  • Connecting rod length (2002 tii homologation 5445): 148 mm centre to centre.
  • Performance cam installation rules (Schrick, apply to any M10 cam):
    • Fit new rockers with a new cam; check that no lobe touches the head.
    • Valve-to-piston clearance at TDC ≥ overlap lift + 1.5 mm (pistons may need valve pockets).
    • Intake and exhaust valve heads ≥ 1.5 mm apart at maximum overlap; retainer ≥ 1.5 mm from the stem seal at full lift.
    • Valve springs ≥ 1.0 mm from coil bind at full lift; set installed height with shims and measure every valve.
    • Turning the engine by hand is not a clearance check; measure.
    • Timing check: set the specified overlap lift (intake opening, exhaust closing) at cylinder 1 TDC, fit and tension the chain, turn the crank 720° and re-measure.

Brakes

  • Keep ALWAYS in mind the brake pistons/wheel brake cylinder to brake master cylinder diameter ratio
  • If you want to install turbo ventilated front discs, you need to buy the turbo wheel hubs (increasing the front wheel track and possibly causing interference with the fender) and install spacers between the two parts of the calliper, as well as the turbo retaining clip for the brake pads
  • The easiest way to install ventilated brakes is the ALPINA A4 discs, which can be mounted to ti/tii struts and hubs with a 7mm spacer between the two calliper parts. Parts are available from a user on the German forum, known as “André Meisel”.
  • The NK brake rotor shields can be modified to fit the 2002 and provide higher airflow due to a spoiler they have. This is a common ALPINA modification
  • Double circuit 4-piston 40mm brake callipers for ti/tii/turbo, double circuit 4-piston 40mm brake callipers for all the others except 1502 were single circuit, with a 2-piston 48mm calliper used
  • Handbrake cables between regular 02 and turbo are different due to the 250 mm drum
  • 22mm wheel brake cylinder needs a brake pressure regulator to retain the correct brake bias
  • The E21 has the same drum as the 2002 turbo. However, the E21 has wheel bolts, not wheel nuts, so it has a hole for an M8 fixing screw. The E21 drum is designed to be balanced with the screw; therefore, if you don’t put the fixing screw in, you will notice a shake in high frequencies (kms/hr). Check this thread (https://forum.bmw-02-club.de/index.php?topic=168313.135)
  • Early and late E21 have different drum backing plates, with early ones having the same P/N as the 02 turbo ones. Check this for possible differences (https://www.bmw2002faq.com/forums/topic/229167-250-mm-drums-from-turbo-on-a-tii/), though not sure about the validity
  • The ti/tii/turbo brake booster uses a dual membrane and therefore is larger in length and smaller in diameter compared to the regular one; however, the force amplification is almost the same. The only real reason to use the “big” one is for dual carburettors with the ti filter-box or injection
  • Brake master cylinder of ti/tii/turbo/late NK is ALMOST identical to E12/E9/E3; however, the later ones do not have the pre-pressure valve for the rear drum brakes, and therefore the rear brake outlet is M10. A solution to that is a) to use an M10x1 outside to an M12x1 inside and use the old pre-pressure valve, or b) to buy an M10 pre-pressure valve from ATE
  • The idea of installing a smaller diameter (e.g., 20mm) BMC in a tii/turbo is wrong. By decreasing the diameter, you increase the pedal travel for the same hydraulic pressure. Therefore, in case of hot brakes or air in the system, you end up pressing the pedal to the floor. Safety first!
  • Don’t expect the brake pedal to have a similar, sensitive feel to a modern car. The pedal is heavier than those, and it should be, as there are no electronics involved (e.g., ABS). The traction limit should be less subtle for a daily driver.
  • Flush the brake fluid every two years to avoid corrosion of the brake components (callipers, MC, etc.)
  • Drum shoes can be adjusted by the eccentric bolts and the handbrake cables
  • Eccentric bolts should be regularly inspected and greased to avoid rust

  • If the car is lowered, roll centre spacers should be installed between the strut and the steering arm (which also holds the ball joint for the control arm). These spacers help restore proper suspension geometry by raising the front roll centre and returning the control arms to a more favourable angle. This improves camber gain during suspension compression, allowing the wheels to maintain better negative camber and a larger contact patch during cornering, while also reducing bump steer and excessive body roll (due to the relocation of the roll centre).
  • For more than 1.5 degrees of negative camber, offset strut bearings should be installed, as interference between the strut and wheel assembly may occur (depending on wheel width and offset). On the BMW 2002, altering camber by changing the inclination of the strut assembly also affects the kingpin inclination (KPI) and scrub radius, since the strut, spindle, and upright form a rigid unit. This is unlike modern vehicles, where the knuckle is a separate component; in those cases, strut inclination changes do not affect KPI. A similar effect occurred when using offset roll centre spacers, which also influence the steering axis geometry, in 2002.
  • For the rear axle, if the vehicle is lowered significantly (typically more than 30–40 mm), camber and toe-in should be re-adjusted. The BMW 2002 uses a semi-trailing arm rear suspension, which naturally increases negative camber and toe-in as the suspension compresses. Excessive lowering amplifies these effects, potentially leading to poor tire wear and handling imbalances. Several solutions exist to mitigate this:
    • Flip the rear subframe mounts — This raises the subframe relative to the chassis (~12-13mm), effectively restoring rear camber and toe closer to factory values. Note that the differential mounts must also be adjusted to avoid introducing drivetrain stress due to altered alignment.
    • Eccentric trailing arm bushings — These allow fine-tuning of static toe and camber. However, they may alter the kinematic behaviour of the suspension during travel. While the effect is likely small, it’s worth considering that you're trading static alignment correction for a potentially altered dynamic response.
    • Adjustable trailing arm mounting kits — These provide the most precise control over alignment but require cutting and welding the rear subframe to install. This is a more complex solution, but it allows for proper geometry correction across a wide range of ride heights. Rear axle, due to semi-trailing arm geometry, has adequate camber gain; therefore, statically, it can be set up to lower values than the front.
  • If coil-overs are installed at the rear axle, reinforcement of the wheel housing should be made, as the factory didn’t account for such concentrated forces at this point
  • Anti-Roll Bars (ARB):
    • Anti-roll bars reduce body roll and improve handling balance by increasing roll stiffness.
    • The front ARB is most effective for RWD cars like the 2002, improving turn-in and reducing camber loss during cornering.
    • Avoid oversizing the rear ARB. It can lead to excessive weight transfer, inside wheel lift, and snap oversteer.
    • Polyurethane bushes are a low-cost upgrade to reduce compliance and sharpen ARB response (minimal though). Replacing ARB drop links and joints with rose joints (spherical bearings) eliminates play and enhances response, especially under high loads.
    • Adjustable ARBs allow fine-tuning of roll stiffness for track or road use.
    • Consider matching ARB upgrades with spring and damper rates; balance is key.
    • Lateral locks are essential to prevent ARB side movement, energy loss, and delay in action otherwise.
  • Basic tuning of the vehicle’s steady state behaviour is achieved by roll stiffness (springs and ARB) and the roll centre’s location:
    • Increasing roll stiffness in an axle will increase the load transferred through this axle (portion of the total weight transfer) and therefore will overload quicker than the other. Increasing the front or decreasing the rear roll stiffness will make the vehicle more prone to understeer, while decreasing the front or increasing the rear roll stiffness will make it more prone to oversteer.
    • Roll stiffness can be controlled by springs and ARBs, but since ARBS act only in roll or bump (decoupled from heave mode), it’s better to tune it by the ARBs
    • Increasing the roll centre height increases the lateral weight transfer on that axle
    • Increasing the roll centre to a point close to the centre of mass decreases the adjustability of the vehicle through the roll stiffness

Differential

A Brief Guide to Differentials:

  • The standard gear ratio is 3.64 (used in the 2002, 2002ti, later 2002tii, and BMW 320 E21).
  • Early tii and 323i E21 have 3.45.
  • 1502/1600-2/1602/1802 have 4.11, while the 1600ti has 3.91 (same as some smaller E21 3-series).
  • You can also install E21 differentials in the 02 series by using the rear cover from the 02 differential.
  • Then you need to create two "hybrid" drive shafts from one 02 and one E21 drive shaft each. To do this: Remove the rubber boot and the circlip, then take off one joint head from each and install it on the other shaft — the splines are the same. Done.

Determining the Gear Ratio:

The number of teeth is stamped on the left side of the differential neck, but it is usually no longer legible after all these years.

Therefore, counting rotations is the fastest and easiest method:

  • Mark the input and both output flanges with chalk lines.
  • Then rotate the input flange (make sure both output flanges turn evenly) and count how many turns are needed until the output flanges make exactly one complete revolution.
  • If the input turns a bit more than 4 times → it's a 4.11 axle.
  • A bit more than 3.5 times → 3.64.
  • A bit less than 3.5 times → it's a 3.45 (early 2002tii).

This method is very reliable.

Limited-Slip Differential (LSD):

People often wonder if a differential has a limited-slip unit. These were available from the factory as optional extras with 25% or 40% lock, and for motorsport use, even with 75% lock. For street use, 25% is usually more than enough.

It is said that the preload in these LSDs was minimal by the factory (https://www.bmw2002faq.com/forums/topic/291034-id-of-lsd-from-exterior/), 50 cmkp=4.903325 Nm. Cmkp is a unit no longer used; it is equivalent to a centi-meter-kilopond.

If you remove the rear cover, you can see the differential carrier. The lock percentage is usually stamped or engraved on it — something like S40 for 40% lock, for example. If it's not marked, you’ll have to scrutinize it — it’s not something you can always tell at a glance.

By the way, there's no simple way to test if a limited-slip unit is still working correctly.

Quick Functional Test (If Diff is Out of the Car):

If you're skilled, you might be able to do a quick test from the outside with the diff removed:

  • Stand the diff on the neck.
  • Grab the two output flanges with both hands.
  • Try twisting them against each other.

If this is very tight, good – it's probably still working. If it turns easily, it may or may not be a limited-slip.

  • Now turn both output flanges in the same direction.
  • The input flange will want to rotate, but it can’t because the diff is sitting on it and you're holding it still.
  • Now, under this preload, try turning the output flanges against each other again.
  • If it's harder now than before, it's a limited-slip. If it feels the same, it’s an open diff.

As mentioned, this requires some feel and experience. It can be totally fine if the output flanges can either be turned easily against each other (when unloaded) or not at all by hand. Compared to the 100+ horsepower applied during real driving, even a firm hand is incredibly weak.

More notes

  • Homologation 5331 lists the standard 3.64 and an optional 4.37 final drive. The Alpina A1 conversion required the 3.64.
  • (forum) Several original 02 LSDs had no Belleville (preload) washers, while E21/E36 small-case LSDs do. Without them both wheels turn freely on a lift like an open diff, so a free-spinning wheel does not prove worn clutches.

Troubleshooting

Brakes

Brake Booster

  • Rough engine running and constant hissing from the brake booster -> Membrane leaking: Replace.
  • Vacuum leaks and poor braking performance -> Replace brake booster
  • Hard pedal pressure - no vacuum or key valve defective ->
  • Check. Pump the brake booster dry and start the engine with the brake pedal depressed. The pressure should now ease and the pedal should sink slightly. If it remains hard, check the vacuum hose and check valve for blockages. If they are okay, the master cylinder valve is stuck – take it to a specialist for repair!
  • A stuck key valve can also occur intermittently. You never know whether the power booster will engage or not when you press the pedal. The fun factor (especially on descents) is considerable, as one unlucky rider experienced on the descent from Mt. St. Agatha (Tuscany, 1,700 m). Intermittent hard brake pressure – it was truly exhilarating.
  • If the master brake cylinder is leaking in the primary section (towards the brake booster), replace it quickly, as the brake fluid will dissolve the diaphragm. This can be seen by moisture at the bottom of the master brake cylinder, where it connects to the brake booster. On these booster models, a rubber sealing ring must also be installed on the master brake cylinder flange, otherwise unmetered air can be drawn in.
  • Check the function of the brake booster
  • Turn off the engine,
  • Press the brake pedal several times until strong resistance is felt.
  • Keep the brake pedal depressed and start the engine.
  • If the brake pedal now gives way, the brake booster is generally functioning. If it doesn't give way, check the vacuum hose and check valve for continuity. If they are okay, the key valve is stuck. Take it to a specialist for repair!
  • The vacuum check valve can be installed backwards if you overlook the printed arrow, which must point towards the source of the vacuum, i.e., towards the intake manifold.
  • Rapid test for CO content: With the engine idling, quickly pump the brake pedal a few times. This introduces additional air via the brake booster. If the engine speed remains constant within approximately 50 rpm, the mixture is around 3% CO, which is within the acceptable range. If the engine speed increases, the mixture is too rich; if the engine speed drops noticeably, the mixture is too lean.

More notes

  • Brake booster, extra vacuum check: run the engine at idle (highest vacuum), switch off, then pull the hose at the booster. A hiss means the booster holds vacuum. The NK booster vacuum system is identical to the 02's. (forum)
  • Bosch idle and CO values (1975 sheet): idle 850–950 rpm on carb models (800–950 Turbo, 850–1000 US tii); CO 3.0–4.0 % on the 1502, ≤ 4.5 % on the Turbo. US models: set CO with the air-injection hose and the charcoal-canister vacuum hose disconnected and plugged.

How it is set up now

  • Engine: BMW M10, 2.0 litres (M10B20 block, not the original engine), 89 mm Mahle pistons (+0.1 mm), E21 2.0 cylinder head, compression 8.3:1.
  • Induction: two Weber 40 DCOE 76/77 under an Alpina air box, since April 2026 (see the project story). Before that, the single Solex carb of a 2002 2.0.
  • Ignition: Bosch 0 231 151 008, the centrifugal-only distributor of the 2002 ti and tii.
  • Gearbox: Getrag 242, 4-speed, Borg-Warner synchros, on an E21 gearbox mount.
  • Differential: ZF 40 % limited slip, 3.64 final drive.
  • Clutch: Sachs 215 mm (late 2002 / E21 size), FAG master cylinder, ATE slave cylinder.
  • Brakes: ti/tii/Turbo brake booster (ATE T51) and 23.81 mm master cylinder. Front: ti/tii struts with 256 mm Zimmermann tii discs, ATE four-piston calipers from a low-mileage BMW 2000 (rebuilt in 2025) and ATE pads. Rear: 250 mm Turbo/E21 drums with 19 mm wheel cylinders. Vented front brakes are being built (see the project story).
  • Suspension: KONI yellow Sport dampers front and rear, 16 mm front and 15 mm rear anti-roll bars.
  • Wheels: 2002 Turbo steel wheels 5.5J×13 with 185/70 R13 tyres. A set of Touring Sportpaket FPS alloys is ready as the second set.
  • Steering wheel: a Petri ti/tii wheel since 24 April 2026; before that a MOMO Niki Lauda.
  • Instruments: a restored 2002 ti "silver dollar" cluster, 220 km/h, since 2 August 2026, with the odometer set to zero at the restoration. Before that the car had an original early 2002 tii cluster, made for the 3.45 differential of the early tii, so with the 3.64 the speedometer read a little high.
  • Body and lights: post-1973 front panel; Hella headlamps and Hella 192 fog lamps.
